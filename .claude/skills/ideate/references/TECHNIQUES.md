# Divergent-thinking techniques

Use at least four different techniques per session, and label each idea with the one that produced it.
The point is range: every technique pushes in a direction the obvious answer does not.

## 1. How might we … (reframe)

Rewrite the problem three ways before generating anything — narrower, wider, and from the opposite side.

- *HMW help authors reuse a character?* → narrower: *HMW keep one character's voice consistent across books?*
  → wider: *HMW let any story element travel between books?* → opposite: *HMW make it safe for a character to diverge?*

Each reframe usually yields its own cluster of ideas.

## 2. Analogy from another domain

Ask how an unrelated field solves the same shape of problem.

- Software: inheritance, forks, packages, version control, feature flags.
- Games: save files, mods, character sheets, fog of war, procedural seeds.
- Film/TV: series bibles, continuity supervisors, story rooms.
- Science, law, cooking, sport, logistics — the farther away, the fresher.

Prompt: *"What would a [domain] person do here, and what is our version of it?"*

## 3. Inversion

Invert the goal, list ways to achieve the inverted goal, then invert each answer back.

- *How would we make continuity errors as likely as possible?* → "no shared source of truth" → idea:
  one shared source of truth.

## 4. Remove a constraint

List what we assume is fixed (one book per project, one author, text only, online only, one language,
LLM is required …), remove one at a time, and ask what becomes possible.

## 5. SCAMPER

Take an existing feature and apply each verb:
**S**ubstitute · **C**ombine · **A**dapt · **M**odify (magnify/minify) · **P**ut to other use · **E**liminate · **R**everse.

## 6. Persona lens

Generate ideas as a specific person would want them:

- a first-time novelist on a phone; a professional writing 5,000 words a day;
- a translator; a reader; a self-hosting operator with no GPU;
- an author writing in Vietnamese or Chinese; a team of co-authors;
- someone who distrusts AI and wants it to stay out of the way.

## 7. Worst possible idea

Deliberately propose the worst solutions, then ask what is *almost* right inside each one.
Removes the fear of saying something silly, and often surfaces the real constraint.

## 8. Extreme scale

Ask what changes at 10×, 1000×, or 1/10 — a 3-million-word series, 10,000 concurrent readers,
one book with one chapter. Scale breaks assumptions and exposes the real bottleneck.

## 9. Time shift

- What would this look like if we built it in 5 years, with far better models?
- What would it look like with no AI at all?
- What would a 1995 desktop app have done?

## 10. Combine two unrelated features

Pick two existing LoreWeave capabilities at random (glossary × translation, knowledge graph × reader,
plan-forge × wiki …) and ask what a feature using both would be.

## 11. Steal from users' workarounds

Look at what people already do by hand, in spreadsheets, or in other tools to cover the gap
(forums and issue trackers from Research are the best source). A workaround is a validated need.

## 12. Constraint forcing

Impose an artificial limit and design within it: *it must work offline*, *it must take one click*,
*it must cost nothing per call*, *it must ship in a week*. Tight constraints produce specific ideas.

## Keeping it divergent

- Do not write "but", "however", or "this would be hard" during Diverge. Save it for Converge.
- If ten ideas in a row are variations of one theme, switch technique.
- The wild ideas are not filler: keep at least three in the final raw list.
